HMDA Loan-Level Records · El Paso County, TX · 2007–2025

El Paso,
underwritten.

Every mortgage application filed in El Paso County leaves a public record: who applied, what they asked to borrow, what the home was worth, and whether the bank said yes. 547,755 of those records, spanning seventeen years and two housing cycles, are behind every chart on this page.

Source: Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA) Loan/Application Registers, filed with the CFPB / FFIEC · Compiled from raw LAR files, 2007–2025 · ffiec.cfpb.gov/data-browser

01 · The arc of a market

From foreclosure crisis
to rate-hike hangover

In 2007, four in ten mortgage applications in El Paso County were denied, the leading edge of a national credit crunch that would define the decade. By 2021, in the middle of a pandemic-era refinancing wave, that denial rate had fallen below one in five. It has since crept back up as interest rates climbed.

The typical loan amount nearly tripled over the same period. Home prices rose sharply, especially after 2018, and rates climbed too, so borrowers needed to finance far more to buy the same kind of home.

05 · Why applications fail

Credit history still leads,
but debt-to-income is catching up

Every denied application carries a reason code. Credit history has been the single largest reason for denial in every year of this data, but its share has drifted down, from about 41% of stated denial reasons in 2007 to roughly 28-31% in recent years. Debt-to-income ratio has moved the opposite direction: from 17% of denial reasons in 2007 to consistently over 28% since 2021, now nearly tied with credit history as the top reason applications get turned down.

That shift lines up with the affordability story above: as required loan amounts climbed faster than incomes, the math of qualifying (not just creditworthiness) became a more common reason to say no.

Stated denial reasons, share of denied applications by year

Each application can list multiple denial reasons; this chart uses only the first (primary) reason listed, so shares reflect the leading cause, not every contributing factor. Applications denied without a stated reason are excluded.
03 · A persistent gap

The gap narrowed.
It didn't close.

Denial rates fell for every racial group tracked in this data between 2007 and 2021, when the pandemic-era refinancing boom lifted approval odds broadly. But the gap between White and Black applicants' denial rates has been remarkably persistent across the full data set: it narrows in good years and widens in tight ones, without ever fully closing.

Two categories worth reading carefully rather than at face value: "Race Not Available" is a data-quality artifact, usually loans purchased from another lender that didn't collect applicant demographics, not a demographic group, and its denial rate shouldn't be compared directly to reported groups. And smaller categories like American Indian/Alaska Native, while showing high denial rates in most years, represent small sample sizes in El Paso County; year-to-year swings there are noisier and less reliable than the White/Black/Hispanic comparisons.

04 · A base with a base

Conventional, FHA, and
a distinctly military VA share

El Paso's loan mix looks different from most Texas counties because of Fort Bliss. VA loans, backed by the Department of Veterans Affairs and available to eligible service members and veterans with no down payment required, made up barely 3.5% of applications in 2007. By 2024 that share had grown sixfold to roughly 22%, one of the highest concentrations of VA lending anywhere in the state, tracking the base's growth into one of the Army's largest installations.

FHA loans, which allow lower credit scores and smaller down payments than conventional financing, tell a related but separate story. Their share spiked during the 2008-2012 credit crunch (peaking near 28% in 2010) as tightening conventional standards pushed more borrowers toward FHA's more flexible underwriting, then settled into a steady 20-24% band that has held since.

Loan type mix by year

Conventional loans are not government-insured; FHA is insured by the Federal Housing Administration; VA is guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs; FSA/RHS (a small, typically under 2% share here) is backed by the Farm Service Agency or Rural Housing Service for rural properties.
06 · Price outrunning paychecks

The gap between a home
and a paycheck

Property values have only been reported since 2018. Before that, HMDA captured just the loan amount, not the home's price. Since 2018, though, the picture is clear: home prices have climbed faster than borrower income, widening the loan-to-income gap even as rates rose.

Median property value vs. median applicant income, 2018–2025

07 · Who applies vs. who lives here

The typical applicant
is not the typical El Pasoan

People who apply for a mortgage are never a random sample of a county's residents; renters, people without stable income documentation, and people who already own their home outright don't show up in this data at all. But the gap is worth sizing. Among 2022-2025 applicants, median reported income was $85,000, about 42% higher than El Paso County's median household income of $59,888 (2024 American Community Survey estimate). Every dot below is one applicant from a random sample of recent records; the dashed line marks the county's actual median household income for comparison.

Split by outcome, the gap narrows for people who get turned down: denied applicants had a median income of $71,000, noticeably closer to the county median than the $89,000 median among originated loans. Denied applicants are still above the typical El Paso household, but meaningfully more like it than the borrowers who get approved.

08 · Tract by tract

Where lending concentrates

Every record carries a census tract, the natural unit for a neighborhood level map. 240 El Paso tracts appear in this data. Census tract boundaries were redrawn twice in this window (after the 2010 and 2020 censuses), so the map below switches boundary files automatically depending on which year is selected: 2000-vintage tracts for 2007–2011, 2010-vintage for 2012–2021, 2020-vintage for 2022–2025.

The 2000-vintage boundary file used here is a simplified cartographic version and covers roughly 84% of tracts that appear in 2007-2011 lending data. A handful of older tract codes have no matching polygon and show as gaps on the map for those years (they're still fully present in the table below).

09 · Notes on the data
How this was built

Records come from two generations of the HMDA loan/application register schema. 2007–2013, 2015, and 2017 use the legacy format (statewide Texas files, filtered here to El Paso County, FIPS 48141); 2018 onward, including 2020, use the modern post-2017-rule format, pre-filtered to El Paso County. 2014 and 2016 are not currently in this archive and appear as gaps in the trend lines.

Race and ethnicity are harmonized across schema versions but not perfectly equivalent: legacy years use a single reported applicant race/ethnicity; modern years use CFPB's derived_race/derived_ethnicity, which can reflect joint applications. Property value was not collected before 2018. Loan purpose "Refinance" combines legacy code 3 with modern codes 31 (refinancing) and 32 (cash-out refinancing). Denial rate is calculated as denied applications divided by all decisioned applications (originated, approved-not-accepted, denied, or preapproval outcomes); withdrawn, incomplete, and purchased-loan records are excluded from the denominator. Race categories with fewer than 5 records in a given year are suppressed from breakdowns to avoid unreliable small-sample rates.

Tract boundary vintages: census tract lines are redrawn after each decennial census, and a tract's shape (and sometimes its ID) can change between vintages even when the underlying neighborhood hasn't. This dashboard matches each data year to the tract vintage HMDA filers were required to use that year: 2000-vintage tracts for 2007–2011, 2010-vintage for 2012 and 2018–2021, 2020-vintage for 2022–2025. A tract's boundary on the map will visibly shift if you compare, say, 2011 to 2012. That reflects a redefinition, not a change on the ground.

Built from raw HMDA Loan/Application Register files for El Paso County, TX (2007–2013, 2015, 2017–2025, excluding 2014 and 2016). All figures are directly computed from these records, with no modeling, imputation, or estimation beyond the medians and rates described in Notes on the Data.
